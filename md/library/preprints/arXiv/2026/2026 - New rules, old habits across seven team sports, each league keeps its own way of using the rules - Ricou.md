# New rules, old habits: across seven team sports, each league keeps its own way of using the rules

                                                   New rules, old habits: across seven team sports, each league keeps
                                                                     its own way of using the rules
                                                                                            Gustavo Pedro Ricou
arXiv:2609.05584v2 [physics.soc-ph] 15 Nov 2026




                                                              School of Computer Science and Statistics, Trinity College Dublin, Dublin, Ireland
                                                                                            pedrorig@tcd.ie



                                                                                                    Abstract

                                                            Studies of a rule change usually report one average effect, for one league or several combined.
                                                        We asked whether a rule change reorders leagues, in 101 leagues of seven sports. When the
                                                        substitution limit rose from three to five, the leagues already ranked from most substitutions
                                                        to fewest, as known for the biggest leagues. In their first season under five, the fourteen top
                                                        leagues kept that order (rank correlation 0.89; one means the same order, zero unrelated orders).
                                                        That is about as firmly as from one ordinary season to the next. A prediction for twelve second
                                                        tiers, written before their records were read, held (0.73). They ranked like their country’s top
                                                        league. Across rule changes in other sports, leagues kept their order in four of eight tests. In
                                                        every sport measured, a league stayed near its place among its sport’s leagues from season to
                                                        season. Under five, clubs inside a league differed more within a season than the leagues did, but
                                                        their differences lasted less. Rule-makers should judge a change league by league, against each
                                                        league’s own seasons.

                                                  1    Introduction
                                                  Every team sport has rules on who may play and when. Football caps its substitutions, volleyball the
                                                  players on its match sheet, handball its team time-outs and cricket each bowler’s overs. Basketball
                                                  and ice hockey let players come and go, and baseball now times its pitchers. These rules change
                                                  often [1, 2]. Yet studies of a change usually report one average effect on results, running, injuries
                                                  or the length of a game, for one league or several combined [3, 4, 5, 6]. Where one rule was studied
                                                  in many countries, its effect differed between them [7, 8]. So an average over leagues can hide how
                                                  each league responds. This paper asks what each league does with the same rule, in seven sports.
                                                      A rule can say what teams may do, and we call this the room it gives them. They choose what
                                                  to do with that room. A speed limit is the everyday example. It sets how fast drivers may go.
                                                  Each road then has its own usual speed below the limit. In the same way, each league uses a rule’s

                                                                                                        1
room in its own way. We call a league’s own way of using the rules its habit. Statisticians call
it a standardised league effect (Supplementary Section S6). A habit is measured from records the
leagues already keep, such as substitutions per match or the number of players a team uses. The
word describes only what teams do. A league’s habit is the sum of its coaches’ decisions. If habits
differ, and keep differing, a rule-maker who reads the first season’s average misjudges the leagues
at both ends.
    Football offers the cleanest case. In the summer of 2020 dozens of football leagues raised the
substitution limit from three to five. IFAB, football’s rule-maker, had allowed five at FIFA’s request,
to protect players’ welfare during the pandemic [9, 10]. Each competition chose whether and when
to use it [9]. So one new permission reached many leagues at dates of their own. In 2022 IFAB
made this permission permanent [10, 11]. Every substitution is recorded with its minute, so what
each league did with the new room can be counted.
    Leagues are known to keep their own profiles, for example in home advantage and in fouls and
cards [12, 13]. Analysts have shown that the biggest football leagues kept the same order in their
use of substitutes from one season to the next [14, 15]. One of them called these differences lasting
and explained them by each league’s customs [14]. This paper tests whether leagues keep their
order through a shared rule change, in football and in six other sports. We wrote most of these
tests down before reading their data. We also wrote down nineteen predictions for seasons, leagues
and competitions before reading their records.
    We tested four claims. First, under five substitutions football’s leagues would use the new room
unevenly and keep their order. Second, leagues in other sports would also keep their order in how
they use their rules. Third, a league’s place among its sport’s leagues would persist from season to
season about as strongly in every sport. Fourth, a coach who moved would carry part of the old
team’s distance from its league’s average. If these held, a rule change would be best judged league
by league, against each league’s own seasons.


2     Methods
2.1   Sports, leagues and records
Table 1 lists the sports in which these claims were tested, the rule change studied in each, and their
leagues and records. Football’s records come from transfermarkt-datasets, a public collection built
from Transfermarkt, a football statistics website. We read its snapshot of 3 August 2026 [16]. The
fourteen European leagues whose substitutions are counted hold 43,159 matches from 2015–16 to
2025–26. Other leagues enter some measures (Table 1).
    Each league’s limit in each season, three or five, was taken from the rules in force and checked
against the substitutions made (Supplementary Table S1). Under five, a top-division team may
substitute in at most three breaks in play, called windows, and at half-time [9, 11]. A season in
which the limit changed is split at the date of the change. We count substitutions per team-match,
which is one team’s side of one match. Supplementary Section S1 shows how the counts were

checked against an independent record, and corrected where the records fell short.
   The records of basketball, volleyball, handball, ice hockey and baseball come from the leagues’
and federations’ own sites. Cricket’s T20 leagues play matches of twenty overs a side. Their records
come from Cricsheet, a public archive of ball-by-ball records [17].

2.2   Ethics
The study reused only public match records that already existed. These records were its only
source on players and managers, so informed consent was outside its scope. Several tests read
information related to health: the injury reasons that the records give for some substitutions, and
players’ absences from matchday squads. Both were counted by league, and only league values are
reported. The records name professional players and managers, so they were treated as personal
data, although they are public. Processing was for scientific research under the research provisions
of the General Data Protection Regulation. Every published table and figure is aggregated to
leagues, or to summaries across managers. The author treated these published records as archival
data, which Trinity College Dublin’s research ethics guidance exempts from approval [18]. So the
study ran under that exemption. The same guidance grants approval only before a study begins.

2.3   What is measured
A rank correlation, or Spearman’s, shows how well two orders agree: one for the same order, zero
for unrelated orders and minus one for the reverse.
   The spread between leagues in a season is the standard deviation of their means: roughly, a
typical league’s gap from their mean. A league’s place on a measure is how many spreads its value
sits above or below the mean of its sport’s leagues that season. The persistence ϕ is the share of
its place a league keeps from one season to the next. So after k seasons, a league keeps on average
ϕk of its place. We call this slow loss of place drift. We measured one ϕ for each sport. We used
the correlations between every pair of seasons, with the chance swings of single games taken out
(Supplementary Section S6).
   A team’s distance from its league is its substitutions per team-match minus its league’s mean
that season. We average it over each coach’s spell. Basketball’s distance uses the five busiest
players’ share of minutes, and baseball’s the players used beyond the starters. The coach’s carry is
the rank correlation between the old team’s distance and the new team’s, across the coaches who
moved.

2.4   Tests and statistics
Each test was written down as a short text, most often a clause of its own (Supplementary Section
S3). The text fixes what the test measures, its direction, which leagues qualify and what each
outcome would be called. Each was saved, with its date, in the project’s private online repository
(Supplementary Section S4).

    The other sports were tested on a split record. Each game went to one of two halves by a fixed
rule that works like a coin toss. Half A was read freely, to choose each measure and pass mark.
The tests were then saved before half B was read. The split guards against choosing a measure
on the luck of single games. But both halves share the same leagues, so a measure chosen to suit
those leagues could pass in both. The strongest evidence is therefore the seventeen predictions for
new seasons, leagues or competitions. Each was written down before any of its records was read,
and each was read once. One was rewritten before its records were read, and it is counted once.
    We call these two kinds of test pre-specified. Football’s own tests, with baseball’s two on
managers, were written during the analysis, after their records had been read for other questions.
Of the 20 behind the claims, seven were saved 6 minutes to 3 hours before they were run. The
other thirteen were written down when they were run, so they describe rather than test.
    A 95% interval gives the values that the data leave plausible (Supplementary Section S1). P
is the chance that luck alone gives a result at least this strong. Most tests are one-sided, because
each fixed its direction in its clause. Such a test passes only on a link in its own direction. Every
analysis added later is labelled stated. It came after the tests, so it describes rather than tests [19].
    Some values combine the leagues of several sports. They come from random-effects models,
which give a combined value and the spread of the leagues around it [20]. They were fitted after
the split’s tests, so their values describe rather than test.

2.5   Sample size estimation and justification
The sources set the sample, so its size followed from them rather than from an estimate made
in advance. Every league that the sources cover deeply enough to carry the measures was read.
In football, fourteen of the source’s thirty-one top leagues have seasons under three substitutions,
so its coverage sets the sample (Supplementary Section S1). That is a justification by the whole
available population [21, 22]. For such a sample, this journal asks for the smallest effect each test
could detect [23]. The unit of each order test is the league, so its sample size is its number of
leagues (Table 2).
    For an order, the smallest effect of interest is a rank correlation of 0.5. Below it, the order in
one season accounts for less than a quarter of the differences in a later one.
    To detect a link, a test needs a 0.80 chance of passing at a one-sided P below 0.05. Twelve
leagues detect only a true rank correlation of 0.69 or more. Fourteen leagues detect 0.65 or more.
So the order and explanation tests could detect only links well above the smallest of interest. The
largest test of coaches who moved detects 0.20 or more. These values come from exact counts up
to nine leagues and from simulation beyond (Supplementary Section S1).


3     Results
The tests open with a check. As expected, each rule moved what it was written to move (Table 2).
The NHL, North America’s top ice hockey league, brought in three-on-three overtime to settle


                                                   4
more tied games before a shootout [24]. Between December 2014 and 2016–17, five European
leagues brought it in too. In them, three-on-three overtime multiplied the odds of settling a tied
game before a shootout by 1.71. FIBA, basketball’s world body, moved its three-point line out in
2010 [25]. The move cut each league’s share of shots from three by about 9.9 per cent of that share.
Baseball’s pitch clocks were written to speed up the game [26]. They shortened games in all four
leagues tested. The size of the line’s and the clocks’ effects differed between leagues (Supplementary
Section S5). As Meyer and Klatt found, football’s two extra substitutions moved playing time from
starters to substitutes [27].

3.1   Football’s leagues kept their order through the change
Under three substitutions football’s leagues sat close to their limit (Figure 1). As earlier counts
found, a team made about 2.85 substitutions a match, close to three in every league [28, 29, 30].
The leagues differed in how often teams used all three, from 76 per cent of team-matches in Scotland
to 95 per cent in Portugal. So the leagues were already in an order, as one analyst had seen in the
biggest leagues [14].
   That order carried through the change. The fourteen leagues’ order over 2015–16 to 2018–19
was much the same as in their first season under five (rank correlation 0.89). Under three, one
season’s order predicted the next season’s at 0.80 to 0.87 (Supplementary Section S2). So the order
held about as firmly as from one ordinary season to the next. These values were read after the
change, so they describe rather than test.
   A prediction then tested the order in twelve European second tiers. It was written before
any of their records was collected. Their order under three predicted their order under five (rank
correlation 0.73, one-sided P = 0.0041). In the seasons under five they also ranked much as
their own country’s top league did. That held for the eight whose top leagues we had read (rank
correlation 0.90, one-sided P = 0.0023), and for all twelve (0.85; Supplementary Section S4). So
the order is partly a country’s, shared by its first and second tiers.
   Under five, the top leagues spread apart in their substitutions, as expected when a limit that
most teams reach is raised [31]. Had each league kept its share of the limit, the spread would have
grown by five thirds. It grew more than that from 2020–21 to 2023–24, and about that much in
the last two seasons (Figure 1b; Supplementary Section S2).
   The change spread the leagues apart, and they kept their order. In the main order test, saved
3 hours before its run, the order in the first season under five predicted the order in 2025–26 (rank
correlation 0.73, one-sided P = 0.0033). On the eight leagues still unread when the test was written,
the rank correlation was 0.57 (P = 0.076). The order from the seasons under three faded slowly. By
2025–26 its rank correlation with the new order was 0.60, as slow drift would give (Supplementary
Section S2).
   We tested four explanations of the order: money, rotation of the starting eleven, the value of
the bench and absences from matchday squads. With thirteen or fourteen leagues, these tests could
find only a strong link, and every link was weaker than that. Inside leagues, richer clubs used about


                                                  5
as much of the limit as other clubs (mean rank correlation +0.02, 95% interval −0.16 to +0.20).
In all, 51 tests read these and other explanations. After a correction for the number of tests, only
one passed: how often teams had used all three windows with substitutions left. It largely restates
how many substitutions a league makes (Supplementary Section S3).

3.2   Leagues in other sports kept their order in four of eight tests
Across rule changes in other sports, the leagues kept their order in four of the eight pre-specified
tests of the split record. Each test read few leagues. So only about four of the eight would pass
if every order held at a rank correlation of 0.7 (Table 2). Across FIBA’s longer three-point line of
2010, basketball’s leagues kept their order in the share of shots from three. Across FIBA’s shorter
shot-clock reset of 2014, they kept it in the five busiest players’ share of minutes and in the number
of players given ten minutes. Volleyball’s leagues kept theirs in the number of players used.
   The other tests failed. Basketball’s pace, a team’s possessions per forty minutes, kept a weaker
order. The 2014 change drew the leagues together in pace, as another pre-specified test expected.
Volleyball’s substitutions a set lost their order. Ice hockey’s and baseball’s tests read four leagues
each, where only a perfect order can pass. Ice hockey’s test read when a trailing team pulls its
goalie, a choice its rules leave to the coach (Supplementary Section S4).
   Predictions also read orders across seasons in these sports, and four of seven held. Baseball’s
eight leagues kept their ranks in pitchers used per team and game. The leagues lay far apart in
pitchers used, so their prediction had expected this with near certainty. Cricket’s thirteen T20
leagues kept their order in how many bowlers a side uses. The limit on each bowler’s overs stayed
the same, and some leagues changed their substitute rules (Tables 1 and 2). In basketball, two of
four such predictions held, and ice hockey’s just failed (Supplementary Section S6).

3.3   A league’s place persists in every sport measured
As expected, in every sport measured, a league kept most of its place among its sport’s leagues from
one season to the next (Figure 2). Goals, points, volleyball’s sets and cricket’s runs kept 0.93 to 0.96
of their place a season, about as much. So leagues keep their character in general [12, 32], and their
habit is part of it. The persistence of basketball, volleyball, ice hockey and baseball is consistent
with one value, 0.969 a season. That value rests mostly on basketball’s leagues (Figure 2a). Later
seasons and new leagues, among them the NBA and the WNBA, gave values close to it, as their
predictions expected (Table 2; Supplementary Section S6).

3.4   Football’s clubs differed more than its leagues within a season
Under five, clubs inside a league differed more within one season than the leagues did, as earlier
charts show [30, 15]. Their spread was about 0.36 substitutions a match, against 0.15 to 0.30
between leagues. But a club kept about 0.52 of its distance from its league into the next season,




                                                   6
and a league about 0.91 of its place. These values allow for the chance swings of single matches
(Supplementary Section S5).

3.5   Coaches carried part of a team’s distance from its league
Coaches who moved carried part of their old team’s distance from its league (Figure 3), as studies
of managers’ styles have found before [33, 34, 35]. Football managers carried their distance when
they moved between clubs of one league, and when they changed league. Those tests were written
down when they were run, so their values describe rather than test. Basketball’s coaches carried
it too, on half B of the split record, and a prediction on their moves since 2019 held (Figure 3).
Baseball’s two tests, saved before they were run, failed on its few moves, so baseball is inconclusive
(Supplementary Section S5).
    The carry follows the coach rather than the club’s choice of coach. In football, a coach’s
distance at the old club correlated only 0.08 with the new club’s own distance in the season before.
Allowing for that earlier distance, the carry was 0.26 in football and 0.31 in basketball. A model
that separates managers from their clubs found a part that is the coach’s own. It was small: about
0.13 substitutions a match, or 4 a season (Supplementary Section S5). These later checks are
stated.


4     Discussion
Football’s leagues kept their order through the change of limit, about as firmly as from one ordinary
season to the next. The twelve second tiers read later kept theirs too. We read seventeen of the
nineteen predictions written before their records were read, and twelve held. Of these passes, seven
could plausibly have failed, among them the second tiers’. The other five would have passed under
a much weaker claim too (Table 2; Supplementary Section S4).
    These results fit how sport science sees a rule. A rule that gives room without saying how to use
it is a task constraint [36]. Loosening a constraint widens the choices that work, and players and
coaches choose among them [37, 38, 39]. In this view a competition organises itself [40]. Football
shows this at the scale of whole leagues. The old limit of three held the leagues close to it. When
teams are held at a limit, the count shows only that they wanted at least that many [31]. So raising
the limit had to let the leagues spread apart. That part of the spread is the limit’s doing. But the
order beneath the limit was already there, and the limit did not set it.
    Two readings fit the kept order. In one, each league raised its old level in proportion to the
new limit. In the other, each league answered the change in its own way. The old limit held most
teams at three, so the leagues’ old levels understate how differently they wanted to use substitutes.
Each league’s level also drifts slowly, even when no rule changes. So its departure from the leagues’
shared rise lasts from season to season (Supplementary Section S2). These counts therefore cannot
tell the two readings apart. The claim here is the one both share: the leagues kept their order
through the change.

   Although clubs differ more within a season, a change is best judged by league. A rule is adopted
competition by competition. The Premier League’s clubs voted three times in 2020 against five
substitutions, partly from concern that five would favour clubs with deeper squads [41, 42]. So the
league is where a change is adopted, and where differences in its use last longer.

4.1   Practical applications
A rule-maker weighing a change, from IFAB to a single competition, can expect the leagues that
use the old rule most to use the new room most. The old order matched the first season under
five at a rank correlation of 0.89, and 2025–26 at 0.60. The overall rise was known only after each
season (Supplementary Section S2). The gaps between the leagues are modest. In each season
under five, the spread between football’s leagues was 0.15 to 0.30 substitutions per team-match.
Over a season of about 33 league matches, that is about 5 to 10 substitutions per club. Clubs
within one league differed more, by about 12 substitutions a season, and a coach’s own part was
about 4 (Supplementary Section S5). A new limit holds teams back first where use is already near
it. In 2025–26 the busiest league used 94.6 per cent of five, and the least busy 82.4 per cent.
   So a change is best judged league by league. Methods for rules adopted at different times
estimate an effect for each group of leagues that adopted it together [43]. Before the change,
measure how each league uses the rule, from the records it already keeps. After it, compare each
league with its own seasons before, not with the average of all leagues. Judge it over several
seasons. After this change, use kept rising: every league made more substitutions in 2025–26
than in its first season under five. Most made about 76 per cent of their rise to 2025–26 in the
first season (Supplementary Section S2). As reviews of rule changes advise, evaluate a change in
several competitions [6], allow for their differences and register the plan publicly before the records
arrive [2]. A study that combines leagues to estimate a rule’s effect on injuries or running should
give each league its own rise in substitutions, season by season. It should also report the effect
league by league.

4.2   Relation to other work
The closest report charted substitutions in seven of these leagues, from the same source, up to
2021–22 [30]. It found them close to three a match under the old limit, and at four to five under
the new, apart from England, still under three. This paper finds them already in an order, and
asks whether it lasted. Another study uses the same change to study coaches’ decisions within
matches [44].
   Several of these results first appeared in the first version of a preprint on nine sports [45]. This
paper is that preprint’s second version. The first version also claimed a law linking a league’s load
management to its timing. This version drops it: the two measures agree because of how they are
built (Supplementary Section S7).

4.3   Limitations
No test here shows cause and effect. Every quantity describes how playing time was recorded, and
a coach’s reasons enter only where a team sheet records them. The records show how teams used
their substitutions, not how often players were injured. No habit here is linked to results, running
or injury. A test of the five-substitute rule’s aim needs each league’s injuries and hours of play,
recorded under football’s consensus methods [46].
   The football records come from one public source, checked against an independent record only
for some leagues and seasons (Supplementary Section S1). In 2020–21 and 2021–22 many records
were incomplete. The rule change also came with a pandemic. But England, which kept three
substitutions through the crowded season of 2020–21, did not use more of them (Supplementary
Section S2).
   Each league also sets rules of its own that were not measured in the other sports. Examples are
roster sizes, limits on foreign players and MLB’s limits on how many pitchers a team carries and
how soon it may change them [47, 48]. These alone could keep a league in its place.
   Averaged over all team-matches, the windows held back at most 0.22 to 0.42 substitutions a
match, by league. So part of a league’s shortfall from five may be the windows. Had every team
they stopped made all five, the order would barely have changed (rank correlation 0.96). The order
was there under three, before the windows (Supplementary Section S3).
   The tests are small. Most rest on four to 17 leagues, so they could detect only a strong link.
The predictions on persistence would have failed only at a much weaker persistence, so their passes
say little (Supplementary Section S6).
   No public register holds the tests. Their dates rest on the project’s private repository, whose
history was rewritten (Supplementary Section S4). For the tests written during the analysis, a date
shows when a test was saved, not when its records were first read [49]. The split record’s tests were
uploaded with their results, so only the order in which they were saved dates them.
   When football’s main order test was written down, the seasons under five of France, Germany,
Italy and Spain had already been read. On the other eight leagues alone it fell short of its pass
mark. It also measures persistence after the change, not across it (Supplementary Section S2).
The second tiers share their country’s order, so they are not an independent sample of leagues.
In five of the eight countries one decision set both tiers’ limit, so what carries the order is open
(Supplementary Section S4).
   Two predictions for a football rule of 2026–27 were not read, because this study ended before the
end of that season (Table 2; Supplementary Section S4). The coach’s carry rests on managers who
moved, and estimates from few movers can be distorted [50]. Supplementary Section S5 discusses
the two published criticisms of the design [51, 52].




                                                  9
5    Conclusion
A speed limit sets how fast drivers may go, and each road has its own usual speed below it.
Football’s substitution limit worked in a similar way. When it rose, every league climbed. Most
leagues made most of their climb in the first season. At first they kept the order they already had,
and that order faded slowly, as drift would give. A prediction written before twelve second tiers
were read found that they kept their order too. In every sport measured, a league’s place persisted
from season to season. A rule sets the room, and each league uses that room in its own way. So
a rule change should be judged league by league, against each league’s own seasons before it, and
over several seasons.
